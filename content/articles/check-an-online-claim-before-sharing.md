An arresting headline or a neatly cropped screenshot can feel ready to pass on. Checking it does not always require an hour of research. It does require a pause between finding something interesting and presenting it to other people as true.

This routine is for ordinary information checks. For consequential health, legal or financial decisions, seek the relevant authoritative guidance and qualified help rather than relying on a social post or a general magazine article.

## Identify the actual claim

Separate the claim from the reaction around it. Is the post saying an event happened, somebody said something or a policy will change? Write it as one plain sentence. If you cannot tell what is being asserted, there may be nothing precise to verify yet.

Notice whether a question is being presented as though it supplies its own answer. A headline that asks whether something might happen is different from evidence that it has happened.

### Find the original source

Follow a quotation, chart or image back to its first available context. Open the full article or statement rather than stopping at a cropped image. Look for dates, location and the name of the body responsible.

If a post refers to research, try to find the original paper or the issuing institution’s explanation. Read what was measured and what limitations are stated. You do not have to resolve every technical question to recognise when the post goes further than its source.

## Compare evidence, not repetition

Several pages repeating the same sentence may all depend on one unverified source. Look for reporting or primary material that adds independent evidence. When a claim concerns an organisation’s own service, its current official page is a useful place to check terms and availability.

[Ofcom’s media literacy resources](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-literacy) provide a route into wider work on understanding online information. They are a starting point for learning, rather than a certification that a particular post is trustworthy.

## Read the date and the missing context

An accurate old story can mislead if it is recirculated as new. Check both the publication date and the date of the event. A photograph can be real while the caption attached to it is wrong.

Look for the full sentence around a quotation and the labelled axes of a chart. If the missing material is unavailable, keep that uncertainty visible instead of filling it with a guess.

## Share less certainty when less is known

You can decide not to forward a claim. If you discuss it, link the source you actually read and explain what remains unclear. Do not describe something as confirmed merely because it feels plausible.

If you already shared an error, correct it where the same audience can see it. Explain the changed fact plainly rather than quietly replacing the link. ## Turn the post into a sentence you can test

A post can combine an event, a reaction and a prediction so closely that it is difficult to tell what needs checking. Separate them. Write the factual claim as one plain sentence, then identify which parts are interpretation or speculation. A statement that a policy has changed needs different evidence from a prediction that it might change later.

Notice the level of certainty in the wording. May, could and is considering do not mean the same thing as has done. When you repeat a claim, keep those distinctions rather than making the account more dramatic. If you cannot identify a precise assertion, it may be better to ask for the original source or wait. The fact that a post provokes a strong response does not make its content sufficiently clear to verify.

## Follow the citation beyond the familiar-looking name

Open the source that is actually being cited. A recognisable institution's name can appear in a post without the institution having made the claimed statement. Check whether the link leads to the original material, a report about it or another page repeating the same summary.

Read enough context to understand what the source says. A headline may simplify an article; an article may simplify a report. If the underlying document is available, look for the relevant passage and its date. Do not claim to have checked a report when you have only read someone else's description of it. You can be transparent about the stage you reached and what remains unresolved. That is more useful than borrowing authority from a source you have not actually examined.

## Ask whether multiple accounts are independent

Several websites using nearly identical wording may trace back to the same original claim. Count the evidence they provide, not simply the number of places where the sentence appears. Look for direct documentation, clearly attributed reporting or another account that adds information rather than copying a summary.

Independence is not a guarantee of accuracy, but repetition alone is not corroboration. If every route returns to an anonymous post, keep that limitation visible. Avoid turning a lack of contradiction into proof. Some claims may remain unresolved because the relevant information is unavailable. In that case, you can decide not to pass the claim on, or discuss it with an explicit explanation that it has not been established by the material you found.

## Check what the date is doing

Read the publication date, the date of the event and any update notice separately. An old article may be accurately written but misleading when presented as a new development. A recent update can also refer to an event that occurred much earlier. Keep the timeline clear when summarising what happened.

The same attention helps with screenshots. A cropped image may omit the date, author or surrounding explanation that changes its meaning. Search for the original page or document where practical instead of relying on the crop. If you cannot recover the context, say so. Do not substitute a plausible date from another account merely to complete the story. A gap in the record should remain a gap until there is evidence to fill it.

## Read quantities and comparisons with their labels

If a claim relies on a chart or number, identify what is being measured, over what period and for which group or place. Check whether a comparison uses the same definition on both sides. A percentage without a clear reference point can be difficult to interpret responsibly.

You do not need to become an expert in every statistical method before recognising missing context. If the relevant definitions or limitations are absent, avoid making a stronger conclusion than the source supports. Look for an accessible explanation from the original publisher. For consequential decisions, use appropriate authoritative guidance and qualified help. An everyday checking routine can improve the questions you ask; it does not turn a general reader into a specialist adviser on every subject mentioned online.

## Use a worked example without inventing an event

Imagine a post says that a local venue has changed its admission arrangements. Before forwarding it, identify whether it refers to general entry, one exhibition or a particular event. Open the venue's current official information and compare the conditions. Check the date and whether the post describes a temporary arrangement.

If the official page is unclear, the accurate result is that the detail remains uncertain. You might contact the venue or tell your group to check before travelling. This example illustrates a method, not an actual change at a named institution. It shows why a small claim can still need careful wording: two pages can appear to disagree while discussing different parts of a service, and a simplified repost can lose the distinction.

## Correct the record where the audience can see it

If you shared something inaccurate, add a clear correction in the place where people encountered it where possible. Identify what was wrong, provide the better source and explain what remains uncertain. Quietly replacing a link can leave the original misunderstanding intact for people who already read the post.

Keep the correction proportionate and factual. You do not need to repeat a misleading quotation in a way that gives it another prominent outing. If the error involved another person's privacy or reputation, be particularly careful about reproducing details unnecessarily. The useful habit is to make your current understanding visible without pretending the earlier mistake never happened. A willingness to revise a claim is part of responsible sharing, not a reason to stop checking things in the future.

## A practical decision worksheet

Use this record when a claim feels important enough to pass on. Keep each answer attached to material you actually examined, and leave unknown details marked as unknown. A clear account of what you checked is more useful than an impressive-looking collection of links whose relationship to the claim has not been established.

| Decision | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Claim | Write one factual sentence that can be examined, separating an event from a prediction, opinion or emotional reaction surrounding it. |
| Original | Identify the earliest relevant material you can access, and distinguish reading the original from reading another person’s account of it. |
| Context | Check the event date, publication date and surrounding explanation, including details omitted by a cropped screenshot or shortened quotation. |
| Independence | Ask whether different accounts supply their own evidence or simply repeat wording from the same unsupported source without adding confirmation. |
| Limits | Record what remains uncertain, missing or beyond your ability to assess, especially where a consequential decision needs appropriate authoritative guidance. |
| Sharing | Decide whether to share, wait or refrain, and keep the wording no more definite than the evidence you have actually checked. |

If later information changes the answer, update the same audience clearly where possible. A checking routine works through revision and evidence; it should not make a first impression harder to correct.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many sources do I need before sharing something?

There is no useful universal count. Consider the quality, relevance and independence of the evidence. Several pages repeating one unsupported claim are not the same as several independent accounts. For an organisation’s own current service, its official information can be the appropriate place to begin.

### Does a professional-looking website make a claim reliable?

Appearance alone does not establish accuracy. Check who is responsible, what evidence is supplied, whether the original material supports the summary and whether the information is current. A polished page can repeat an error just as easily as a poorly designed one.

### What should I do if I cannot find the original source?

Keep that limitation visible and avoid presenting the claim as confirmed. You can ask for a source, wait for better information or choose not to forward it. Do not fill missing context with a plausible explanation simply because the claim seems likely.

### Can an old article still be worth sharing?

Yes, if you make its date and context clear and do not present it as a new event. Check whether subsequent developments affect the point you want to make. An accurate historical account can become misleading when the surrounding message implies it describes what is happening now.

### What if I already shared an error?

Correct it where the same audience is likely to see the update. State the changed fact and link the source you actually checked. Avoid quietly editing away the original context or making a new claim more certain than the evidence allows. A clear correction is more useful than defensive wording.
